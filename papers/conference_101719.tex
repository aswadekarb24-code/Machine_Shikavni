\begin{abstract}
Automated Traffic Sign Recognition (TSR) is essential for autonomous driving and advanced driver-assistance systems. However, environmental corruptions such as fog, snow, blur, and sensor noise can drastically reduce classification reliability. This paper presents a three-phase progressive study evaluating and improving the robustness of four CNN architectures -- a compact custom CNN (TrialCNN, 0.68M parameters), ResNet-18, MobileNetV2, and EfficientNet-B0 -- against 14 distinct environmental and digital corruption types across severity levels 1 to 5, following the taxonomy of Hendrycks and Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019robustness}. All models are trained from scratch on three benchmark datasets -- GTSRB, BTSD, and CTSD -- spanning different geographies and class counts.

\textbf{Phase~1} (RGB augmentation pipeline) established a baseline revealing that deeper, pretrained-style backbones do not inherently generalize better to unseen corruptions.
\textbf{Phase~2} introduced a \emph{Robust Preprocessing Shield} -- a grayscale, median-filtered, CLAHE-equalized input pipeline -- yielding up to $5.3$ percentage-point improvement in mean corrupted accuracy.
\textbf{Phase~3} extended this with a corruption-specialist ensemble: 32 specialist models (4~architectures $\times$ 8~preprocessing variants, each tailored to a specific corruption family) are combined via a confidence-weighted mean of softmax probabilities, producing a 32-member \emph{Ensemble-32} that achieves \textbf{88.0\% mean accuracy} under all 14 corruption types -- a $2.6$ point improvement over the best single specialist model (TrialCNN/Motion: 85.4\%) -- with near-perfect clean accuracy (BTSD~97.8\%, CTSD~100.0\%, GTSRB~99.7\%). These results demonstrate that targeted, corruption-family-aware preprocessing combined with ensemble fusion is a highly effective and architecture-agnostic robustness strategy for small-resolution traffic sign recognition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Autonomous vehicles rely heavily on vision-based traffic sign recognition to safely navigate complex environments. While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) achieve high performance on standard, clean test benchmarks, their accuracy often degrades severely when exposed to real-world visual distortions such as weather conditions (fog, snow, frost), capture artifacts (motion blur, defocus blur), and digital noise (Gaussian, shot noise, JPEG compression).

A natural question for practitioners is whether larger, ImageNet-style backbones are worth their additional computational cost when the end task is small-resolution sign recognition rather than general-purpose image classification. A second, equally practical question is whether robustness can be systematically engineered through \emph{preprocessing design} rather than relying solely on architectural capacity or data augmentation during training.

To probe both questions, we conduct a three-phase progressive study. We benchmark four architectures of increasing sophistication -- a compact custom CNN, ResNet-18~\cite{resnet}, MobileNetV2~\cite{mobilenetv2}, and EfficientNet-B0~\cite{efficientnet} -- against 14 synthetic environmental corruptions across 5 severity levels on three traffic sign datasets. We then progressively improve robustness through preprocessing: first with a denoising shield (Phase~2), then with a corruption-specialist ensemble (Phase~3).

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A three-phase progressive robustness study covering 4~architectures, 3~datasets, and 14~corruption types at 5~severity levels each.
\item A \emph{Robust Preprocessing Shield} (grayscale + median filter + CLAHE) that improves mean corrupted accuracy by up to 5.3 pp over an RGB baseline at zero additional model cost.
\item A corruption-specialist ensemble framework: 8 targeted preprocessing transforms, each optimised for a specific corruption family, producing 32~specialist models combined via confidence-weighted fusion to achieve 88.0\% mean corrupted accuracy.
\item An empirical finding that a compact, non-pretrained CNN (TrialCNN) consistently dominates larger transfer-learning backbones in robustness, and that this advantage amplified by specialist preprocessing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Stallkamp et al.~\cite{gtsrb} introduced the German Traffic Sign Recognition Benchmark (GTSRB) and showed that CNN-based classifiers can exceed human-level accuracy on clean, well-curated traffic sign images, establishing GTSRB as the de facto standard benchmark for the task. They demonstrated that spatial pyramid matching and CNN-based features substantially outperformed hand-crafted descriptors, a result that motivates training from scratch on domain-specific data rather than relying on ImageNet representations.

Subsequent work extended traffic sign recognition to other geographies and modalities: Zhang et al.~\cite{ctsd} proposed a YOLOv2-based real-time detector for Chinese traffic signs and released an expanded Chinese Traffic Sign Dataset (CTSD), while the Belgium Traffic Sign Dataset (BTSD) has been widely used to study recognition under varied European road and lighting conditions~\cite{btsd_timofte}. The Belgian dataset is particularly challenging due to the combination of a high class count (62~classes) and a limited number of training examples per class. Sarwatt et al.~\cite{btsd} further studied adversarial robustness across Chinese, Belgian, and German datasets, highlighting that robustness properties of TSR systems are highly dataset- and attack-dependent.

Separately from adversarial robustness, Hendrycks and Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019robustness} proposed a standardized taxonomy of common, naturally occurring image corruptions at five severity levels and showed that classification accuracy on ImageNet degrades substantially even without an adversary. We adopt this corruption taxonomy -- rather than adversarial perturbations -- because it better reflects the passive, non-malicious degradations that an onboard camera encounters during ordinary driving.

On the architecture side, He et al.~\cite{resnet} introduced residual connections to enable training of substantially deeper CNNs, Sandler et al.~\cite{mobilenetv2} introduced MobileNetV2's inverted residual and linear bottleneck design for efficient inference, and Tan and Le~\cite{efficientnet} introduced EfficientNet's compound scaling of depth, width, and resolution. All three are now standard choices for transfer learning in vision tasks; however, their relative corruption robustness when trained \emph{from scratch} on small, low-resolution sign crops has not been directly compared, which is part of the gap this paper addresses.

Ensemble methods have long been used to improve classification reliability~\cite{ensemble_dietterich}, and mixture-of-experts approaches allow specialist sub-models to collaborate on diverse inputs~\cite{jacobs1991adaptive}. Our Phase~3 applies this principle at the \emph{preprocessing level}: each ensemble member is a specialist for a particular corruption family rather than a different classifier for the same input space.

\section{Datasets and Noise Taxonomy}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Benchmark Datasets}
Our experiments utilize three primary public traffic sign datasets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GTSRB}~\cite{gtsrb}: 43~classes, 39{,}209 training and 12{,}630 test images. The largest and most widely studied benchmark; relatively straightforward due to its large training set per class.
    \item \textbf{BTSD}~\cite{btsd}: 62~classes, 4{,}591 training and 2{,}534 test images; the highest class count and the fewest training examples per class, making it consistently the most difficult dataset in our results.
    \item \textbf{CTSD}~\cite{ctsd}: 58~classes, 4{,}931 training and 1{,}233 test images, captured under varied urban lighting conditions in China.
\end{itemize}
All three datasets use class-balanced weighted cross-entropy loss because class distributions are imbalanced, particularly in BTSD and CTSD. The checkpoint with the highest weighted test F1-score is retained for evaluation.

\subsection{Environmental Distortion Categories}
Fourteen visual corruptions from the Hendrycks and Dietterich taxonomy~\cite{hendrycks2019robustness} are synthetically injected into the test sets at 5~discrete severity levels ($s \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noise Corruptions:} Gaussian Noise, Impulse Noise, Shot Noise.
    \item \textbf{Weather Corruptions:} Snow, Fog, Frost.
    \item \textbf{Blur Artifacts:} Motion Blur, Zoom Blur, Defocus Blur.
    \item \textbf{Digital \& Lighting Alterations:} Brightness, Contrast, Pixelate, Elastic Transform, JPEG Compression.
\end{enumerate}
Models are trained exclusively on clean (uncorrupted) data; corruptions are applied only at evaluation time.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Model Architectures}
\label{sec:archs}
We compare four CNN architectures (Table~\ref{tab:architectures}, Figs.~\ref{fig:arch_trialcnn}--\ref{fig:arch_efficientnet}). None use ImageNet-pretrained weights; all are trained from scratch so that differences in robustness reflect architectural inductive bias rather than pretrained feature reuse.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TrialCNN} (custom baseline): a compact three-block CNN with $25\!\to\!50\!\to\!75$ channels, batch normalization, ReLU activations, max-pooling after each block, and a 512-unit fully connected head with dropout ($0.2$--$0.3$). Designed to be a lightweight but strong baseline.
    \item \textbf{ResNet-18}~\cite{resnet}: residual CNN with skip connections, adapted to single-channel input.
    \item \textbf{MobileNetV2}~\cite{mobilenetv2}: depthwise-separable CNN using inverted residual bottlenecks, designed for efficient inference.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNet-B0}~\cite{efficientnet}: compound-scaled CNN combining depthwise-separable MBConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation attention.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phase 1 — RGB Augmentation Baseline (\texttt{train\_tform})}
\label{sec:phase1}
The initial preprocessing pipeline resizes images to $32{\times}32$, applies random affine augmentation (${\pm}10^{\circ}$ rotation, ${\pm}5\%$ translation, $0.9$--$1.1\times$ scale), color jitter (brightness and contrast ${\pm}0.2$), and normalizes to $[-1,1]$ per channel. Training used RGB three-channel inputs with no explicit denoising. While this pipeline benefits from geometric regularization, it leaves the network fully exposed to pixel-level noise and lighting artifacts, producing a strong clean-data baseline but large robustness gaps.

\subsection{Phase 2 — Robust Preprocessing Shield (\texttt{tform\_robust})}
\label{sec:phase2}
We subsequently adopted a \emph{robust preprocessing shield} applied identically at training and evaluation: each image is (1)~resized to $32{\times}32$, (2)~converted to grayscale, (3)~denoised with a $3{\times}3$ median filter to suppress shot, impulse, and Gaussian pixel noise, and (4)~contrast-equalized with Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE, clip limit~$2.0$, tile grid $4{\times}4$) to normalize fog-, brightness-, and contrast-type degradations. The output is a single-channel float tensor normalized to $[-1,1]$. All four architectures' first convolutional layers are adapted to accept a single input channel.

\subsection{Phase 3 — Corruption-Specialist Ensemble}
\label{sec:phase3}

\subsubsection{Specialist Preprocessing Transforms}
The key insight driving Phase~3 is that different corruption families respond to different denoising strategies. We define 8 specialist preprocessing transforms, each designed to best suppress a particular corruption family:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Noise} (\texttt{noise\_tform}): Grayscale, $3{\times}3$ median blur, followed by bilateral filter ($d{=}3$, $\sigma_{c}{=}\sigma_{s}{=}40$) to suppress high-frequency impulse and Gaussian noise while preserving edges.
    \item \textbf{Light} (\texttt{light\_tform}): Grayscale, percentile-based stretch to $[2,98]$ percentile range, followed by CLAHE (clip~3.0) to normalize brightness and contrast shifts.
    \item \textbf{Weather} (\texttt{weather\_tform}): Grayscale, $3{\times}3$ median blur, CLAHE (clip~2.0) to handle snow and frost overlays.
    \item \textbf{Fog} (\texttt{fog\_tform}): Local contrast normalization via Gaussian-difference (LCN, $\sigma{=}1.25$) to remove the low-frequency veil fog adds, followed by percentile rescaling.
    \item \textbf{Defocus} (\texttt{defocus\_tform}): Gaussian pre-smoothing ($\sigma{=}2.0$) + CLAHE; trains the model to rely on low-frequency structure that survives defocus blur.
    \item \textbf{HeavyBlur} (\texttt{heavyblur\_tform}): Heavier Gaussian pre-smoothing ($\sigma{=}3.0$) + CLAHE, complementing Defocus for severe blur cases.
    \item \textbf{Motion} (\texttt{motion\_tform}): Richardson-Lucy deconvolution with a Gaussian PSF ($\sigma{=}1.5$, 15~iterations) to partially reverse the motion-blur degradation.
    \item \textbf{Generalist} (\texttt{generalist\_tform}): Reproduces the Phase~2 shield (grayscale + median + CLAHE), serving as the ensemble's all-purpose fallback.
\end{itemize}

Note: two additional specialist variants (\texttt{blur\_tform} and \texttt{distort\_tform}) were evaluated in an ablation sweep but dropped from the final ensemble after finding they did not improve overall performance; their checkpoints are retained in the repository.

\subsubsection{Specialist Training}
For each combination of (architecture, dataset, preprocessing variant), one specialist model is trained on \emph{clean} images only, using the variant's preprocessing as the fixed input transform. Training uses 100~epochs, AdamW (lr~$1{\times}10^{-3}$, wd~$1{\times}10^{-4}$), cosine-annealing LR schedule, mixed-precision AMP, and batch size~256. The checkpoint with the highest weighted test F1 is retained. This yields $4 \text{ archs} \times 8 \text{ variants} \times 3 \text{ datasets} = 96$ specialist checkpoints.

\subsubsection{Confidence-Weighted Ensemble Fusion}
At inference, all 32 specialist models (4~architectures $\times$ 8~variants) are evaluated for every input. The ensemble prediction is a \emph{confidence-weighted mean} of softmax probability vectors:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{ens}}(x) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{32} w_k(x)\, P_k(x)}{\sum_{k=1}^{32} w_k(x)}, \quad w_k(x) = \left(\max_c P_k(x)\right)^4
\end{equation}
where $P_k(x)$ is member $k$'s softmax output and $w_k(x)$ is its confidence (max probability) raised to the power~4, so that highly confident members dominate. Crucially, this is a \emph{blind setting}: every member always runs regardless of input type. No corruption detector or corruption-specific router is used (a fog-gating approach was explored but left as future work).

\subsection{Training Setup (all phases)}
All phases use AdamW (lr~$1{\times}10^{-3}$, wd~$1{\times}10^{-4}$), cosine-annealing LR, AMP, batch size~128--256, and class-balanced weighted cross-entropy loss. The checkpoint with the highest weighted test F1 is retained throughout.

\subsection{Noise Robustness Evaluation Protocol}
For each trained model and dataset, we evaluate on the held-out test split corrupted by each of the 14 corruption types at each of the 5 severities independently (70 corrupted test passes per model--dataset pair). For every corrupted test pass we record accuracy, weighted F1-score, precision, and recall. The full 32-member ensemble (Ensemble-32) is evaluated by running all member preprocessing pipelines on the test images and fusing probabilities as in Eq.~(1).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Architecture vs.\ preprocessing.} Across all three phases, larger architectures do not translate into better corruption robustness at $32{\times}32$ resolution without ImageNet pretraining. TrialCNN consistently outperforms all three transfer-learning backbones in mean corrupted accuracy. We attribute this to three factors: (1)~deeper architectures may discard fine spatial detail (digit shapes, pictogram edges) that distinguishes sign classes at low resolution; (2)~the robust preprocessing already performs significant denoising work, narrowing the advantage deeper architectures would otherwise have in learning noise-invariant features; and (3)~MobileNetV2's depthwise-separable convolutions are disproportionately sensitive to pixel-level perturbations, consistent with efficiency--robustness trade-offs reported broadly in the literature~\cite{hendrycks2019robustness}.

\textbf{Specialist preprocessing as a robustness lever.} The Phase~3 results confirm that matching a model's preprocessing to its expected corruption family is highly effective. The preprocessing transform often contributes more to robustness than the architecture choice: the TrialCNN/Motion specialist ($85.4\%$ avg) outperforms EfficientNet-B0 with the generalist transform ($74.4\%$) by over 11~pp. This suggests that for deployment scenarios where the dominant corruption type is known (e.g., fog on coastal roads, motion blur from a vehicle-mounted camera), deploying a specialist model is more efficient than scaling up to a larger architecture.

\textbf{Ensemble complementarity.} The 2.6~pp gain of Ensemble-32 over the best single specialist reflects genuine complementarity: confidence-weighted fusion allows better-adapted specialists to dominate on their target corruption family while falling back on the generalist members for out-of-distribution inputs. The blind setting (no corruption detector) is a deliberate design choice; a corruption-aware router could further improve results (see Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\textbf{Fog as the persistent outlier.} Fog at high severity fundamentally degrades spatial frequency content in ways that none of eight preprocessing transforms fully recovers. The local contrast normalization in \texttt{fog\_tform} is effective at moderate severities but fails at the most extreme fog levels (severity~5). This is consistent with results in general image restoration literature where physical fog models require depth estimation beyond the scope of a classification pipeline.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Several limitations bound the scope of our conclusions:

\textbf{No corruption-aware training.} All models are trained on clean data; evaluating corruption-augmented training (mixed or probabilistic corruption injection during training) is a natural next step and may close the residual robustness gap, particularly for fog and motion blur.

\textbf{Blind ensemble.} The current ensemble runs all 32~members on every input regardless of the actual corruption type. A lightweight corruption classifier could route inputs to the relevant specialist members and likely reduce the performance gap for fog and motion corruptions.

\textbf{Dropped specialist variants.} The \texttt{blur\_tform} and \texttt{distort\_tform} members were dropped after a sweep because they did not improve overall accuracy; their checkpoints are retained on disk. Revisiting them with architecture-specific hyperparameter tuning, or using them in a corruption-routed setting, remains an open direction.

\textbf{Grayscale discards color cues.} The robust preprocessing shield discards color information that could be informative for certain sign categories (e.g., red prohibition vs.\ blue mandatory signs). An ablation isolating grayscale vs.\ color preprocessing for individual classes is left for future work.

\textbf{From-scratch training only.} All experiments use models trained from scratch. Repeating the comparison with ImageNet-pretrained initialization would clarify whether the robustness advantage of the compact CNN is specific to the from-scratch, low-resolution regime or persists more generally.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
We presented a three-phase progressive robustness study for traffic sign classification under 14 environmental and digital corruption types. Starting from an RGB augmentation baseline (Phase~1), we introduced a Robust Preprocessing Shield -- grayscale + median filter + CLAHE -- that improved mean corrupted accuracy for TrialCNN by 5.3 percentage points at no architectural cost (Phase~2). We then developed a corruption-specialist ensemble framework of 8 preprocessing variants $\times$ 4 architectures $=$ 32~specialist models combined via confidence-weighted fusion (Phase~3), yielding a final \textbf{Ensemble-32 with 88.0\% mean corrupted accuracy} and near-perfect clean accuracy ($>$97.8\% across all datasets), outperforming the best single specialist model by 2.6~pp.

The central message of this work is that \emph{targeted preprocessing diversity is a more effective and efficient robustness lever than raw architectural capacity for small-resolution traffic sign recognition}: matching each model's input transform to its expected corruption family, and combining the specialists via confidence weighting, extracts corruptionrobustness substantially beyond what any single model -- compact or large -- achieves alone.
